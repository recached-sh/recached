### 3.1 The stream lock is not where writers wait

An outside writer takes its keys' write-order stripes first, and a 10,000-key transaction holds all 256 from before phase 1 until after its replication call. A writer reaches the stream lock only between transactions, when no group is being published: 15–42 contended acquisitions per second, all short. **The longest stream-lock hold is 3.8 ms** (one group of 10,000 frames in P; 3.3 ms in px; mean 1.2–1.5 ms, about 150 ns per frame), inside the transaction's own stripe hold, paid by the transaction. The worst holds are wall time and include preemptions on CPU 1, which a primary worker shares with the replica.

### 3.2 Two kinds of stripe, one contended

**Write-order stripes** (256 `tokio` mutexes) are held by EXEC from before phase 1 to after phase 2; every writer's wait is there. **Store stripes** (the `KeyValueStore`'s own) are held by phase 1 only, and a writer touches them only after it holds its write-order stripes, i.e. after the transaction released them: no writer waits on a store stripe in this cell. (A replica's readers do wait on a group's store stripes: 6O-I §7, decision D6.)

### 3.3 What the stripes see

The transactions hold every stripe 98.6–98.7% of the time in all three builds; writers get in during the hand-off gap, which is the same in every build (0.13–0.14 ms), with nearly all four writers queued and waiting when it opens. In the diag builds, writer throughput is the same in H, P and px (490, 487, 505 ops/s). The production builds show a writer loss that the diag builds do not reproduce (§5).

### 3.4 What lengthens the transaction

P holds the stripes 0.45 ms longer than H: phase 2 +0.23 ms (the batched publish, §4) and phase 1 +0.23 ms. px's phase 2 is *shorter* than H's (one lock hold and one offset reservation per transaction, frames handled one at a time), and its phase 1 is P's. Phase 1 is code 6O-I did not change, and its allocator costs are the same in H and P (§4.3), so its +3% is not the allocator. It is the same in P and px, so it is not the publish either.

### 4.4 Allocator-only ablations (`et`, `et2`)

| jemalloc thread cache | transactions/s | CPU per transaction | user instructions per transaction | outside writer ops/s | primary RSS, window mean |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| default (`et`) | 0.936 [0.931–0.954] | 1.028 | 1.081 [1.080–1.083] | 0.883 | 1.407 |
| 16,000 slots per size class (`et`) | 0.934 [0.910–0.944] | 1.022 | 1.077 [1.074–1.078] | 0.892 | 1.303 |

- **With no thread cache, P is H:** the same instructions per transaction to three decimals (99.0 M each), transactions and writers within noise, RSS +6%. With nothing cached there is nothing for the batch order to miss, so every P–H difference the default configuration shows lives in the thread cache's interaction with the batch.
- **A larger cache alone does not help the instructions** (`et`): jemalloc's incremental GC flushes cached objects between a thread's publishes (each of the two clients' transactions runs on its own task, every other transaction). With the GC effectively off as well, three quarters of the extra instructions go (+1.5 M against +6 M per transaction) and transactions recover to 0.968×.
- **RSS under these settings:** P's own window RSS stays at 115–126 MiB in all four settings; the P/H ratio falls (1.41 → 1.06) because **H's** RSS rises (82 → 89 → 97 → 118 MiB) as its objects sit in larger caches or churn through the arena. So these settings say nothing new about P's extra RSS; its mechanism rests on jemalloc's dirty pages (`e7b`), decay 0 (`e1d`) and px (`V0.6O-I8-MEMORY-INVESTIGATION.md` §3).
- These are controls, not recommendations: turning the cache off costs every build 36% more instructions (99.0 M against 72.6 M per transaction).

### 6.3 Transaction size (`e4`: 2 reps × 60 s per size, provisional; the same cell otherwise)

| P against H, paired (both reps) | transactions/s | CPU per transaction | user instructions per transaction | outside writers | primary RSS, window mean |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 writes | 1.016, 1.020 | 1.016, 1.007 | **1.017, 1.016** | 1.018, 1.018 | 1.014, 1.017 |
| 1,000 writes | **1.049, 1.053** | 0.970, 0.974 | **1.020, 1.020** | 1.017, 1.026 | 1.231, 1.240 |
| 5,000 writes | 0.959, 0.947 | 1.017, 1.018 | **1.053, 1.055** | 0.966, 0.898 | 1.337, 1.333 |

I tracks P at every size (`raw/e4/summary.md`).

- **The cost scales superlinearly with the transaction's size**, as a thread-cache overflow should: jemalloc's per-thread cache holds about 200 objects per size class, so a batch of 2 × 100 objects mostly fits and 2 × 10,000 overflows a hundredfold. Instructions are +1.7% at 100 writes, +2.0% at 1,000, +5.4% at 5,000 and +8.2% at 10,000.
- **Below a few thousand writes P is faster than H** (1,000 writes: 1.05×): one stream-lock hold and one offset reservation per transaction save more than the allocator costs. The crossover lies between 1,000 and 5,000 writes.
- **RSS grows with the size** (+1.5% at 100 writes, +23% at 1,000, +33% at 5,000, +40% at 10,000) and returns to H's level after the load at every size. In 60 s windows P's RSS at 1,000 writes was still rising slowly at the end (58 → 75 MiB in 40 s, then 76–79 MiB over the next 40 s), so `e4b` measured that cell for **300 s** (2 reps, H and P): P's 30-second means held 75.6–78.2 MiB through the whole window in rep 1 (slope −0.02 MiB/min) and rose from 70.2 to 75.1 MiB in rep 2, flattening toward rep 1's level (74.3, 73.8, 74.4, 74.6, 75.1 over the last 150 s); H held 60.5–62.3 MiB. After the load P returned to 58.8–59.0 MiB (H 58.2). P's transactions were 1.075× H's (`raw/e4b/`).

- **1,000-write transactions:** px keeps P's gain over H (1.04–1.05×) and brings instructions and RSS to H's level.
- **2-write transactions with a replica:** P and px were 0.96× of H in these two 60 s reps; **`ex3`, the same cell over 3 reps × 120 s with H, I and P, finds it flat**: P 0.991× [0.981–1.007] of H's transactions, CPU per transaction 1.001× (I: 0.991×, 0.999×), as 6O-I's campaign B did (1.002×). What remains is what 6O-I measured and D6 accepted: the replica's CPU per transaction +10% (a 2-frame group costs a replica one assembly and one store pass), primary RSS +3 MiB (5.6%), and +3% primary instructions at equal CPU (`raw/ex3/`). The 60 s result was the shorter windows' noise.

## 7. The experimental build px

px is P with `publish` rewritten as a loop that, for each frame of the transaction in turn, copies it, wraps it, pushes it onto the backlog, trims the backlog, and queues it to every replica (`raw/px-patch/px.patch.txt`, about 120 lines in `replication.rs` and one call in `server_state.rs`). Unchanged: one hold of the stream lock for the whole transaction, consecutive offsets, the group in the side table, the header on the first frame, the byte and frame budgets, and every resulting state.

`ex` (3 reps × 120 s), paired against H:

| | P | px |
|---|---|---|
| transactions/s | 0.954 [0.921–0.978] | **1.026 [0.992–1.046]** |
| user instructions per transaction | 1.083 [1.082–1.084] | **0.998 [0.997–0.998]** |
| primary CPU per transaction | 1.035 [1.017–1.043] | **1.005 [0.986–1.014]** |
| primary RSS, window mean | 1.344 [1.308–1.387] | **0.980 [0.963–1.010]** |
| primary RSS after the load | 0.970 | 0.994 |
| replica CPU per transaction | 0.841 | **0.844** (the gain of applying a group at once is kept) |
| outside writer ops/s | 0.881 [0.873–0.940] | 0.889 [0.739–1.007] |

- px removes the extra instructions, the extra RSS and the transaction-rate loss. It matches or slightly exceeds H in transactions because its replication call is cheaper than H's per-frame one (§3: 1.21 against 1.48 ms), which offsets the phase-1 residual it shares with P (+0.21 ms, §3.4).
- **Outside writers:** px's writers are 0.889× of H's in `ex`, 1.064× in `e1q` and 0.974× in `e6`: px restores the part that follows the transaction rate, not the per-hand-off residual (§5).
- **Semantics:** review B checked the patch: trimming after each push reaches the same backlog suffix (by induction), and a replica whose budget runs out is dropped at the same prefix. The patch's test, `a_group_published_frame_by_frame_leaves_the_same_state`, compares 300 random publishes with an independent model after each; review B asks for a differential test against P's `publish` covering the frame cap, a closed channel and a frame larger than the backlog before any PR. The server suite on px: `V0.6O-I8-CORRECTNESS-VALIDATION.md` §2.
- Proposal: `V0.6O-I8-RELEASE-GATE.md` §3. Not opened, not merged.

## 8. Superseded: diag v1 (`raw/e5/`)

The first diag builds counted every stream-lock acquisition in a shared counter (once per frame in H, once per group in I/P). More importantly, a diag *replica* timed its ordering stripes and its apply once per frame in H (about 900,000 times a second) and once per group in I/P. H's diag replica, already near its CPU limit, fell behind and resynchronized 9–14 times per run (review A C1, review B F1), so every H-diag number in `raw/e5/` comes from another regime: its writers were 1.7× faster, its hand-off gap 0.33 ms. Diag v2 removes the shared counters, and `e7b` runs each diag primary with its label's production replica. The I/P diag v1 numbers agree with v2 in direction only (P phase 1 7.28 against 7.08 ms; writers per hand-off 4.55 against 5.06); the heap profiles in `raw/e5/` (allocation sites and requests, equal in H and P) are unaffected, since they count requests rather than time.
