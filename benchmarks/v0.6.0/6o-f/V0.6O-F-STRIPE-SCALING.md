# v0.6.0 Phase 6O-F3: stripe contention and scalability

| | |

## 1. Two synchronization layers

| | Server: replication-order stripes | Store: write-order stripes (6O-D) |
|---|---|---|
| Where | `ReplHub::write_order` (`replication.rs`): `Vec<tokio::sync::Mutex<()>>`, about 40 B each, **unpadded** (neighbours also share hardware cache lines) | `KeyValueStore::write_order` (`store.rs`): `std::sync::Mutex<()>` + `seq` + `multi`, one 64-byte line each |
| Held | **Across awaits** (AOF, replication, notifications), and by a transaction for its whole queue. **Exclusive even for a transaction's read keys** | Synchronously, inside one store call |
| Hash | SipHash-1-3 (`DefaultHasher`) `% 256` | Multiplicative hash, top 8 bits |
| Must match the other? | No: the hashes are independent | No. It must be a multiple of the 16 index shards (`index_shard` = stripe % 16) |
| Stripe sets | `transaction_stripes`: a bitset of `N/64` words, then **a scan of all N bits** to build the list (§2.1) | `SmallVec` per write |
| All stripes taken by | SAVE/snapshot, replica attach (full-sync cut), promotion, expiry sweeper (per chunk), eviction tick, capped writes that may evict, **every EXEC on a capped store**, EXEC with FLUSHDB/KEYS/SCAN/DBSIZE, QSUB's initial state | `FLUSHDB`, restore/replace |
| All-stripe implementation | `lock_every_stripe`: N tokio locks in sequence. Tokio's cooperative budget forces a yield about every 128 acquisitions while the taken stripes are held (§3) | a loop over `std` locks |

"False sharing" below means two unrelated keys hashing to one stripe.

| Writers only (no transactions) | — | — | 1.007 | 1.002 | Control |
| **E** `DBSIZE, GET` (whole keyspace) + 4 writers | **0.645** | **0.392** | 0.891 | 0.750 | §3 |

**Note on `x256` against `base`.** The instrumentation changes the contended path:
- **Lock path.** In release builds `base` calls `lock().await`. The patch calls `try_lock` first, then `lock().await`, and in tokio 1.54 only `lock().await` spends cooperative-scheduling budget.
- **Counters.** It adds about 7 relaxed atomic adds and 3 clock reads per transaction.
- **Result.** `x256` against `base` is 0.967–0.995 in workload D (all 5 repetitions) and 1.001–1.020 in workload E.

So stripe-count ratios use `x256`, which shares the lock path at every N. The generator used up to 0.80 of its 3 CPUs in the uncontended cells, so absolute tx/s may be generator-limited there. The ratios compare labels under identical load.

### 2.1 Workload D: what the counters show

| Per 30 s cell (median) | 256 | 512 | 1,024 | 1,024s |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Stripe acquisitions | 14.9 M | 14.9 M | 14.6 M | 14.7 M |
| …that found the stripe held | 28,238 | 13,372 | 6,557 | 6,622 |
| Total time waiting for a held stripe | **684 ms** | 147 ms | 59 ms | 132 ms |
| Writer ops refused the unordered fast path (gate closed by an EXEC) | 134,103 | 139,022 | 131,933 | 132,518 |
| Lock time per transaction | 2.01 µs | 2.03 µs | 2.20 µs | 2.22 µs |

- **For 2-key transactions, false sharing is measured and small.** Collisions follow k/N: they halve with each doubling. At 256, 684 ms of waiting over 54 connections × 30 s is **0.04% of connection time**, across about 7.3 million transactions and 280,000 writer operations. That **refutes, at `410235b`, the mechanism 6O-E proposed** for its −18% transactions and −34% writers on unrelated keys. **What did cause 6O-E's figures remains unknown.** They compared isolation against no isolation (`1f9232f`) with 10 s × 3 cells, and isolation itself is not under study here.
- **The unordered gate is a hypothesis, not a finding.** About 48% of writer operations found the gate closed by an EXEC and took the ordered path, and that count does not depend on N. A flat count shows only that the gate does not vary with N, not that it limits writers. Three facts argue against a simple gate explanation:
  - the gate closed on every EXEC before 6O-E too (`V0.6O-E-PERFORMANCE.md`), so it cannot explain 6O-E's change;
  - on a capped store, writers never take the unordered path, yet they ran at 89k/s beside capped transactions (§3.1);
  - writers alone ran at 155k/s.

  Writer throughput here is set mostly by **how the 3 workers' time is shared** between 50 transaction connections and 4 writer connections. An ablation build would be needed to test the gate. This study did not run one.
- **Per-transaction lock time grows with N.** `transaction_stripes` scans all N bits to build the stripe list. At 256 that is already about 2,000 user instructions per transaction, about 12% of a 2-key transaction; at 1,024 it adds about 6,100 more. That and cache pressure are why 1,024 is 2–5% slower here. Iterating only the set words would remove most of it **at any N, including 256**: a cheap optimisation worth taking whatever the stripe decision.
- **2,048** was measured in the screen only (3 × 10 s). Its ratios against `base`, 1.008, 0.941 and 0.911, are within that screen's spread (`x256`: 1.084, 0.999, 0.874). No claim is made for it beyond the barriers (§3).

### 2.3 Workload A, `MGET×10`: wide read-only transactions do collide

At 256 the 11-key read-only transactions exclude **each other**: a transaction's read keys take the same exclusive stripe Mutex as its writes.

| `MGET×10` | 256 | 512 | 1,024 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Acquisitions that waited | 5.40 M of 61.5 M (8.8%) | — | — |
| Total wait per 30 s cell | 470 s of 1,500 connection-seconds (**31%**) | — | — |
| Lock time per transaction | 83.6 µs | 8.6 µs | 2.9 µs |

This is heavy false sharing. The 0.04% of §2.1 holds for 2-key transactions only.

The test is closed-loop at saturation: the 3 workers stay busy either way, so throughput does not change, and by Little's law neither does mean latency. The latency distribution does change:

| `MGET×10` latency | 256 | 512 |
|---|---:|---:|
| p99 (repetition ranges do not overlap: 728–757 vs 473–483) | 738 µs | 478 µs |
| p50 | 240 µs | 262 µs |

Below saturation, the waits would show as **up to about 74 µs lower mean latency per transaction** at 512. The waits measured at saturation include queueing, and contention is lower on a less loaded server. A saturated benchmark cannot show that benefit.

**Not evaluated:** a shared (read) mode for a transaction's read keys, with an `RwLock`-style stripe. Read-only transactions would stop excluding each other at any N, without slowing the barriers.

### 2.4 Workloads added after review (`raw/stripes/results-extra-*`, 5 × 30 s, against `x256`)

**Wide write transactions:** `MGET×10, MSET×10` over unrelated keys (20 keys per transaction), 50 clients, plus 4 two-key MSET writers.

| | 256 (`x256`) | 512 | 1,024 | 1,024s |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Transactions/s | 78,259 | 107,096 (**1.372**; 1.290–1.376) | 121,630 (**1.552**; 1.517–1.571) | 122,368 (1.566) |
| Transaction p99 | 6,275 µs | 3,864 µs | **1,263 µs** | 1,259 µs |
| Lock time per transaction | **567 µs** | 346 µs | 111 µs | 111 µs |
| Concurrent writer ops/s | 36,927 | 26,114 (**0.704**) | 6,116 (**0.165**) | 6,198 (0.168) |
| Server CPUs busy (of 3) | 2.20 | — | 2.42 | — |
| Key operations per second (20 per transaction + 2 per writer op) | about 1.64 M | about 2.19 M | about 2.45 M | about 2.46 M |
| Mean latency, transactions / writer ops (Little's law) | 639 / 108 µs | — | 411 / 654 µs | — |
| Writer ops that found the unordered gate closed | 100% | 100% | 100% | 100% |

**Wide write transactions are the class that false sharing does hurt at 256.** Each transaction holds 20 stripes for its whole queue (8% of the table), so transactions exclude each other: 567 µs of lock time per transaction.

**At 256 the server leaves capacity idle** while transactions wait on each other: 2.20 of 3 CPUs busy. More stripes put that capacity to work: 2.42 CPUs at 1,024, and **net work rises** from about 1.64 M to about 2.45 M key operations per second. Server CPU per operation (transactions plus writer ops) is unchanged, 19.1 against 18.9 µs.

**The writers' "loss" is a share effect of a saturated closed loop**, not lost capacity. At 256 the writers were favoured because transactions were stuck waiting: 108 µs mean writer latency against 639 µs for transactions. At 1,024 the two get roughly equal service: 654 against 411 µs. That is mean latency by Little's law, the reliable reading at saturation. Below saturation neither class would wait much. The writer ratio is therefore given little weight in §7.

The gate is closed for every writer op in this workload at every N, because the transactions write. That count follows the writer rate and does not explain the shift.

**Workload D with an AOF, `RECACHED_AOF_SYNC=everysec`** (every writer ordered, frames appended under the stripes):
- **How the AOF was enabled.** `stripe-experiment/stripes.sh` sets `RECACHED_AOF_PATH` for a cell exactly when `SERVER_ENV` contains `RECACHED_AOF_SYNC` (setting the path is what turns the AOF on), and `extra.sh` passed `SERVER_ENV="RECACHED_AOF_SYNC=everysec"`.
- **The AOF files are gone:** they were deleted with each cell's temporary directory.
- **So AOF being on rests on the committed scripts.** It is consistent with the data: transaction lock time is 0.69 µs here against 2.01 µs without an AOF, because no writer holds an unordered pass.

| | 512 | 1,024 | 1,024s |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Transactions | 1.008 | 0.984 | 0.973 |
| Writers | 0.986 | 0.949 | 0.938 |
| Waits per 30 s (256: 286 ms) | 124 ms | 64 ms | 55 ms |

The same as without AOF: no gain for 2-key transactions.

Firefox was running on the host (about 7% of one CPU) during these two runs. The comparisons are interleaved and rotated, so every label saw it equally.

## 3. Whole-keyspace barriers (`raw/stripes/results-barrier/`, 3 repetitions)

All-stripe acquisition time (median; range where it is wide):

| Barrier | 256 | 512 | 1,024 | 2,048 | 1,024s |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `SAVE`, idle server | 14 µs | 23 µs | 42 µs | 76 µs | 38 µs |
| `SAVE` under transaction load | 124 µs | 216 µs | 383 µs | 676 µs | 446 µs |
| Expiry sweeper chunk, under `SET … PX 200` load | 183 µs | 270 µs | 419 µs | 657 µs | 415 µs |
| Replica full-sync cut (**one barrier per run**) | 21 (16–166) | 33 | 84 | 394 (111–1,170) | 268 (79–855) |

- **Idle server:** the barrier grows linearly with N, about 37 ns per stripe (SAVE idle).
- **Under load:** the times are mostly waits for each stripe's holder, and they grow faster.
- **Full-sync row:** one sample per run, with a very wide spread. It is shown for completeness and supports no trend; 1,024s against 1,024 there is noise.
- **`SAVE`:** its wall time (140–170 ms) is the snapshot's own work, and the barrier is under 1% of it.
- **Sweeper:** it ran fewer, longer chunks at higher N. SET throughput under expiry was 159.8–166.6k/s at every N. `DBSIZE` at the end was about 94k, against about 30k live keys expected: the sweeper lagged expiry at every N. That lag is outside this study.
- **The scaling is partly this implementation's.** `lock_every_stripe` takes N tokio locks in sequence and yields about every 128 to the scheduler while holding those already taken. A different barrier would scale differently: one global barrier word, a `try_lock` fast path, or an unbudgeted acquisition. The conclusions below hold for the current barrier.

### 3.1 Capped stores (`raw/stripes/results-barrier2/`, 3 repetitions)

**Writes that cannot evict** take only their own stripes (`execute_locked_write_within_capacity`). With a cap above the dataset, SET ran at 154–157k/s at every N, with zero all-stripe barriers (`results-barrier`, `capped-set`).

**Two capped paths take every stripe:**

| Capped path (per-repetition ratio against `x256`, range) | 512 | 1,024 | 2,048 | 1,024s |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Evicting SET** (`RECACHED_MAX_KEYS=50000`, `allkeys-lru`; 2,000,000 SETs over 100,000 keys; 1 worker, c50) | **0.751** (0.750–0.767) | **0.507** (0.500–0.515) | **0.308** (0.305–0.314) | 0.507 |
| **EXEC on a capped store** (`GET, SET` unrelated, c50 + 4 writers, 3 workers, 20 s) | **0.658** (0.657–0.660) | **0.411** (0.411–0.425) | **0.238** (0.237–0.243) | 0.413 |

- **Absolute values at `x256`:** the evicting SET runs at 55,121 SET/s. The capped EXEC runs at 33,468 tx/s, with 89,384 writer ops/s and a p99 of 1,783 µs.
- **Eviction happened in every repetition:** `DBSIZE` was 50,000, and about 965,000 of the 2,000,000 SETs took every stripe. In the capped-EXEC test, all-stripe acquisitions equal the transaction count.

**Per-operation time is a fixed cost plus a per-stripe cost:**

| | 256 | 512 | 1,024 | 2,048 | Per stripe |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Evicting SET (1/throughput) | 18.5 µs | 24.1 µs | 36.0 µs | 59.1 µs | about 23 ns |
| Capped EXEC (1/throughput) | 29.9 µs | 45.4 µs | 72.7 µs | 125.6 µs | about 53 ns |

So at 256 the all-stripe barrier is already a large share of a capped operation:
- about 32% of an evicting SET (23 ns × 256 = 5.9 µs of 18.5);
- about 45% of a capped transaction (53 ns × 256 = 13.6 µs of 29.9).

This is the clearest result of the study: **on capped stores, more stripes cost directly and heavily.** Multitenant deployments require a cap.

**An existing cost at 256: capped transactions are globally serialized.** Every capped EXEC takes all 256 stripes.
- **The mix.** The capped-EXEC test ran 33k transactions plus 89k writer ops per second, against 230k transactions plus 8.5k writer ops for uncapped workload D. The mix moved to the writers, so the gap in total operations is **1.9×**, not the 7× a transaction-only comparison suggests.
- **The wait.** The 1.46 ms per acquisition is mostly **queueing**: 50 clients serialized on one barrier (Little's law: 50 ÷ 33.4k = 1.49 ms). Taking 256 locks on an idle server costs 14 µs.

This is not a 6O-F regression: `410235b` and 6O-E have it. It is recorded for the release gate.

**Not measured:** fewer stripes (128) for capped stores. The data above suggests they would help capped stores and hurt wide transactions (§2.3). That is a separate question.

## 6. Per-key ordering (feasibility, not implemented)

- **Shape:** a lock table keyed by the key (a sharded map of `Arc<tokio::sync::Mutex>`), allocated on first use and removed when idle. A multi-key write sorts its keys by bytes before locking, for deadlock freedom.
- **Cost per write:** a hash-map insert or lookup, plus an `Arc` allocation per contended key, plus cleanup. The keyed lookup is already the largest single cost on the write path; a comment inside `run_write_gated` (`store.rs`, about line 4556 at `410235b`) puts it at about 21% of a SET. It would also need a second map beside the keyspace, kept consistent across expiry, eviction and RENAME.
- **Memory:** bounded by keys in flight, not the keyspace, if entries are removed when their last waiter leaves. That removal is itself synchronization on the hot path. Phase 6P's target (≤1.5× Redis per key, now 1.88–1.98×) leaves no room for per-key metadata in the entry.
- **All-key barriers** (snapshot, sweeper, full sync, capped EXEC) would need a separate global mechanism.
- **Benefit:** for 2-key transactions false sharing is 0.04% of connection time (§2.1). It is real for wide transactions: read-only ones wait 31% of connection time at 256 (§2.3), and write transactions of 20 keys spend 567 µs per transaction in lock waits (§2.4). Per-key ordering would remove that waiting. But a shared read mode (for the read half), or more stripes once the all-stripe barrier is cheap, recovers most of it without a second keyed map on every write.

**Not recommended.**

## 7. Decision gate (brief §5.9)

| Criterion | 512 | 1,024 | 2,048 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Improves unrelated-key contention reproducibly | **Only for wide write transactions**: +37% tx, p99 −38%, but concurrent writers −30% (§2.4). 2-key transactions: no (D 1.000 / 1.012; with AOF 1.008 / 0.986) | Wide write transactions +55%, p99 −80%, writers −83%. 2-key: no (0.976 / 0.952) | Not measured for wide transactions; 2-key: no (screen) |
| 2. No material harm to ordinary commands | yes | yes | not measured |
| 3. Acceptable whole-keyspace barrier cost | **No**: barriers +48–75%; capped evicting SET 0.75, capped EXEC 0.66 | **No**: 0.51 / 0.41 | **No**: 0.31 / 0.24 |
| 4. Reasonable memory | yes | yes | yes |
| 5. Preserves lock order | yes | yes | yes |
| 6. Compatible with the transaction design | yes, but phase 1 of a capped or `FLUSHDB` transaction takes every store stripe, and its stall grows with N | as 512, worse | worse |

**Recommendation: keep 256 for the server layer, for now.**
- The data is not uniformly against more stripes: wide write transactions gain a lot, and net work rises (§2.4).
- **The deciding cost is criterion 3** on capped stores, which every multitenant deployment uses: direct and global, with tight ratios (evicting SET 0.750–0.767, capped EXEC 0.657–0.660 at 512).
- **Once the all-stripe barrier is cheaper** (follow-up 3), reconsider **512 or 1,024**: the data favours 1,024 for wide transactions (+55% against +37%), and 512 is the smaller change for the barriers.
- **Another option, not evaluated:** a startup setting for the server stripe count. The table is already a `Vec`; `transaction_stripes`' fixed bitset would need a small change. Uncapped deployments with wide transactions could then choose more stripes without capped stores paying for it. It needs a measurement and a configuration decision.

**For the store layer, 256 is provisional.** Under the transaction design's phase 1, a store-stripe collision blocks an OS worker thread (`std::sync::Mutex`), not an async task. §2.1's 0.04% does not carry over, and 6O-G must measure it.

**Follow-ups worth more than a stripe-count change:**
1. `transaction_stripes` iterating set words instead of all N bits: about 12% of a 2-key transaction at 256.
2. A shared read mode for transactions' read keys (§2.3).
3. A cheaper all-stripe barrier for capped stores, where it is already 32–45% of a capped operation at 256 (§3.1).
4. Narrowing the unordered gate's closure for transactions, **only if an ablation confirms** it limits writers (§2.1, hypothesis).

## 8. Limitations and uncertainty

- **Spread:** repetition spreads are 1–8% in most cells (`summary*.md`). Differences under about 3% are not claimed.
- **Workload C** is mode-confounded (§2.2).
- **Generator:** it was up to 80% busy in the uncontended cells. The workload D counters are absolute and do not depend on it.
- **2,048:** measured in the screen (3 × 10 s) and the barriers only, by the brief's rule ("only if 1,024 is promising").
- **Not run:**
  - below-saturation (open-loop) latency;
  - 128 stripes;
  - an ablation of the unordered gate;
  - the store layer under 6O-F2's phase 1 (it does not exist yet).
- **Added after review** (§2.4): wide write transactions (`MGET×10` + `MSET×10`) and workload D with AOF `everysec`. Their writer-loss mechanism is a plausible reading, not a tested one.
- **Provenance of the capped-store data:**
  - The first `barrier2` attempt (04:30–04:32Z) aborted on a script error. The second measured an orphaned server left on its port by the first, so it was discarded.
  - The reported run is the third, 2026-11-25 04:34:15Z–05:29:36Z. These are the **filesystem modification times** of the original `meta.txt` (written at the start) and `barrier2.log` (last written at the end) in the bench directory; neither file contains a timestamp, and the copies here carry the later copy time. `f-driver.log` records only the first attempt.
